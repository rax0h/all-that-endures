# Human & Civilizational Causality — Research Record

**Project:** All That Endures (ATE)  
**Status:** Active research, first evidence-backed tranche — **not** approved gameplay formulas or an implementation authorization  
**Started:** 2026-10-11  
**Game 1 scope:** Approximately 1,500 simultaneously living people, a complete kingdom-scale playable setting  
**Design authority:** [Living Design Document](../LIVING_DESIGN.md), especially Sections 49 and 53–56  
**Change discipline:** Keep empirical finding, theoretical interpretation, modeling hypothesis, and implementation/test evidence distinct. Stage 0.5 is not changed by this research.

> **Question:** What must be true of people, resources, relationships, institutions and circumstances for a small, enduring society to produce its own history—including meaningful love, loss, discovery, prosperity, violence, peace and exceptional lives—without a plot director?

## 1. Research rules

1. **Look for causal mechanisms, not story templates.** The simulation should not schedule a charismatic leader, a war, a famine or a heroic climax. It should permit the conditions that can produce—or prevent—those outcomes.
2. **Do not treat a disputed social-science model as universal human law.** Record counterevidence, domain limits, sample bias and cultural variation. Fantasy species, magic and invented ecologies may change mechanisms; they do not license a predetermined moral psychology.
3. **No invented quantitative constants.** The ~1,500-person target does not imply a validated crop yield, workforce composition, household size, marriage rate, "kingdom viability" threshold, war probability or profession distribution. All such numbers require evidence, setting assumptions and calibration.
4. **Individual state must have consequences.** Food and labor, ability and access, debts and stockpiles, family obligations, rights, institutions and learned skills cannot reduce to decorative NPC biographies. The world must be able to record both attempts and consequences.
5. **No population cheat.** The generated world's people, goods, skills and capabilities must come from its actual physical/biological/magical state. Do not hide external millions or conjure specialists, supplies, recruits or imports unless such external entities/resources are genuinely represented.
6. **One authority, bounded resolution.** Agency chooses context-informed attempts; existing economic, environmental, social, profession and magic systems resolve outcomes; events preserve historical truth. Research must not create a second narrative decision engine.
7. **Test absence as well as emergence.** A peaceful century, unrealized talent, failed expedition or unbuilt institution may be correct. We should understand why those outcomes occurred and distinguish them from dysfunctional stagnation.

## 2. First findings: evidence, what it supports, and limits

The following are **summaries of published arguments and findings**, not proof that a given game mechanic is correct. Sources are linked in Section 9.

| ID | Evidence-backed finding or claim | Why it matters to ATE | Caution / uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| E01 | Historical household research argues domestic labor, craft production, family roles and economic valuation interact with the wider society; households are not merely passive adaptation units [R01]. | Treat households as producers, consumers, caregivers, teachers and political actors; household change affects markets and society. | Evidence concerns particular historical and archaeological settings; do not prescribe universal household/gender roles. |
| E02 | Models of seasonal agrarian societies show production risk can motivate surplus and food storage, while population-level constraints limit how well storage buffers shortages [R02]. | Model seed set-aside, spoilage, consumption, stockpiles, bad seasons and food access; high storage does not mean invulnerability. | Published models are context-dependent and not a universal calorie/yield table. |
| E03 | Archaeological work documents varied skilled adaptations and material practices in medieval rural households, including mixed cultivation, food processing and other household production [R03, R04]. | "Farmer" is not a single job producing generic food; work also includes tools, livestock, gardens, preservation, family labor and household exchange. | Avoid projecting one medieval region onto all cultures, species, biomes or magic economies. |
| E04 | Cumulative culture research highlights teaching, social learning, networks and distributed knowledge; models also study division of labor and specialization [R05, R06]. | Apprenticeship, lost expertise, learned craft techniques, shared tools and collaboration must have persistent consequences. | Competing models and historical contexts differ; invention is not a simple function of headcount. |
| E05 | Ostrom's common-pool resource research identifies recurring institutional features associated with durable collective resource governance [R07, R08]. | Waterworks, grazing, fishing, roads and forests need negotiable access, locally appropriate rules, monitoring and dispute handling. | This is **not** a fixed "eight principles = successful kingdom" recipe; original domain is commons governance. |
| E06 | Self-determination theory finds competence, autonomy and relatedness useful in explaining motivational conditions and well-being [R09]. | People can seek mastery, valued work, belonging, recognition and autonomy—not just money or combat. | A theoretical lens, not three exhaustive NPC motives nor a mechanical utility formula. |
| E07 | Experimental work reported group performance associations with social sensitivity and turn-taking, not merely a single top performer [R10]. | Coordination, communication and experience together could make an adventuring party or workshop effective. | Subsequent reanalysis disputes the general "collective intelligence factor" [R11]. Do not implement a universal group-IQ stat. |
| E08 | Fearon's rationalist account explains how costly war can occur despite mutually beneficial bargaining opportunities due to private information/misrepresentation and commitment problems [R12]. | Conflicts require situated beliefs, bargaining, threats, changing capabilities and inability to trust an agreement. | Designed for interstate bargaining; local raiding, feuds and coercion also need additional anthropological study. |
| E09 | Jervis's security dilemma explains how defensive measures can be perceived as threats and create reciprocal escalation; defense/offense conditions affect cooperation [R13]. | A guard levy or newly fortified road might reassure one community and frighten another without a prewritten war arc. | Not every arms buildup produces violence; geography, institutions and signals matter. |
| E10 | IPCC AR6 assesses climate as a contributor to conflict risk through food/water/livelihood pathways, with important non-climatic drivers and limited evidence for simplistic attribution [R14, R15]. | Drought, disease and resource shocks alter exposure, incentives and capacity. Possible responses include cooperation and adaptation. | No "drought -> war" deterministic rule. Some transboundary water scarcity evidence favors cooperation. |
| E11 | Models of small-population demography find viability depends on mortality, fertility, relationship/mating patterns and chance; environmental variation and demographic chance affect survival [R16, R17]. | At 1,500, age structure, family networks, birth/death timing and shared shocks may matter materially. | These are models, not direct estimates of Game 1 kingdom sustainability; hunter-gatherer settings are not medieval farming societies. |
| E12 | A widely cited methodological critique finds psychological studies often rely on WEIRD participants, limiting confidence in cross-cultural universals [R18]. | Simulate cultural variation and individual experience; never encode modern Western norms as species-wide defaults. | Human cross-cultural evidence cannot simply be mapped to biologically distinct fantasy peoples. |
| E13 | Cultural-evolution models show specialization and exchange can sometimes produce stable stratification, depending on how learning and exchange are structured [R19]. | Markets, skill access and the distribution of surplus may shape hierarchies without a "class hierarchy spawn" command. | This is a mathematical model with explicit assumptions, not a universal history of inequality. |

**Interim synthesis:** A small kingdom's viability is less about an arbitrary number of residents than about whether **people can reproduce, acquire necessities, manage shocks, exchange and learn, settle disputes and preserve enough coordination to continue**. A person can be outstanding and still lack opportunity; an institution can be effective without a famous leader; hardship can trigger cooperation as readily as conflict. These sentences are **ATE synthesis/hypotheses**, not direct quotations or universal empirical laws.

## 3. What an actual 1,500-person kingdom has to sustain

### A. Material reproduction and provisioning

People need nutrition, water, shelter, heat/fuel where relevant, clothing, tools and ongoing care. Different species/ranks/abilities may modify those requirements only when their canon rules support it.

Proposed causal accounting:

- **Production:** Who grows, gathers, raises, fishes, mines, processes, preserves, crafts or magically produces actual goods; which inputs, seasonal windows, tools, land, knowledge and labor are needed?
- **Consumption:** Which individuals/households consume goods, for what needs, and with what changing requirements (age, health, activity, species and magical physiology)?
- **Storage and loss:** Who owns supplies, where they are stored, spoilage, theft, disaster, planned reserves, seed stock, debts, access rights and distribution.
- **Distribution:** Household self-provision, gifts, reciprocal exchange, wages, barter, markets, rents, taxes, rationing, charity and appropriation—only where supported by the actual economy.
- **Bottlenecks:** The mill, a ferry, an irrigation canal, a blacksmith, a caravan, a particular healer, specialized knowledge or an unusually productive farm can become genuinely consequential.
- **Recovery:** Repair, training replacements, altered production, migration, shared provision, costly adaptation or failure.

A settlement that appears affluent while consuming more goods than it can produce, acquire or draw from justified stores has a broken material model. Similarly, unlimited food obtained via untracked caravans would defeat the game's explicit scarcity and consequence rules.

**Unresolved world-boundary question:** Is Game 1 a self-contained kingdom whose normal material supplies and population are entirely represented, or can it trade with *represented and budgeted* neighboring systems? A map-boundary abstraction must not become a free source of goods, people, knowledge or labor. This question cannot be answered by archaeological papers alone.

### B. Population continuity and household variation

Track living age structure, births, deaths, kinship, care dependency, marriage/partnership where relevant, household formation, separation, succession, migration and unequal exposure to hazards. Do not force a constant 1,500 people at all dates.

**Consequences of small numbers:** The death of one teacher can remove an entire chain of training; several births or deaths in one family change inheritance and labor; a localized plague can simultaneously reduce food production and caregiving. This is an **engineering hypothesis to test**, not a promise that every person is always structurally unique.

Do not import an animal-population extinction formula directly into human kingdom gameplay. Nor infer a universal human minimum population from a single forager model [R16]. Culture, alliances, transport, ecological supports, opportunities to meet partners, migration and magic matter.

### C. Economic specialization and access

People need not all be specialist farmers, nor should the simulation assign one fixed population percentage to chefs, merchants or soldiers. Skill specialization depends on surplus, training, individual goals, tools, social access, local demand, material geography and viable exchange.

**Proposed test:** Reduce the viability of a transport route. Who can no longer sell, learn or repair? Who substitutes local production? Who emigrates, goes into debt, changes profession or reorganizes the route? A nominal "trade penalty" is inadequate unless it changes the people and things that actually depend on travel.

### D. Social reproduction and knowledge

Families, teachers, apprentices, schools, institutions and peer networks transmit practices imperfectly. Skills may be learned, improved, combined, monopolized, lost, imitated or independently rediscovered.

A chef training an apprentice, a farmer teaching irrigation and an adventuring party practicing tactics should all use a comparable **causal architecture for learning and transmission**, while preserving their distinctive domain mechanics. An expert may die before teaching everything; a talented apprentice may innovate beyond their mentor. No assumption of "perfect skill inheritance."

### E. Governance, legitimacy and collective action

A kingdom is not automatically a modern state scaled down to 1,500. The **kingdom** name is a legitimate game-setting choice, but the size and structure of its actual political authority must emerge from history and design. It may have a ruler, council, families, religious or magical authority, local customary norms and uneven enforcement capacity; do not manufacture a staffed ministry, standing army and nationwide bureaucracy without enough people and resources.

For each shared resource or obligation, ask: **Who has standing? Who benefits? Who bears the costs? Who monitors? Who may object? Who arbitrates? Who can enforce? Who remembers?** Cooperation can arise without a central ruler; central power can help, harm or be irrelevant depending on context [R07, R08].

### F. Safety, violence and the choice not to fight

Violence involves willingness and ability, social support, equipment, logistics, information, opportunity costs, power asymmetry and risk. Small conflicts can harm society through destroyed crops, absent workers, broken trust and generational grievance, not just casualties.

A **100–200-participant battle** would represent approximately **6.7%–13.3% of a 1,500-person entire living population**. It is a conceivable *exceptional* total participation, not a standard militia size, combat-capable population estimate or target battle quota. The proportion participating in conflict depends on age, geography, supply, health, obligations, personal agency and magical power.

Peace is an outcome with causes, too: credible commitments, workable dispute resolution, repeated cooperation, mutual dependence, credible defenses, trusted relationships or lack of mobilization. Study both escalation and restraint.

### G. Environmental and magical ecology

Environmental hazards are not quest prompts. Production yields, health, animals, monsters, weather, fires, floods, access routes and magical resource ecology affect real possibilities and vulnerabilities. The same rain deficit can cause mild adjustments in one place and dangerous shortages in another due to infrastructure and institutions.

Magical people are neither exempt from causality nor ordinary humans with renamed damage stats. A magical healer may change survivability, a water controller might transform irrigation, and a high-rank combatant may change deterrence; each still has *actual* skills, access, time, material requirements, limits and subjective information. The rank ecology must remain causally and quantitatively plausible at Game 1 population size.

## 4. Research-to-mechanism translation register (candidate, not approved mechanics)

|---|---|---|---|---|

| H02 — Scarcity affects more than price | Local supply/demand, location, ownership, relationships, transport | Substitute, hoard, share, bargain, migrate, steal | Named exchanges, changing supplies, relationships and moves | Global scalar raises "unrest" without material propagation |
| H03 — Skilled specialists create dependencies | Learned skill, tools, patrons, apprentices, throughput, local demand | Train, work, repair, delegate, retire | Named clients, production quality/capacity, bottlenecks | Losing the only miller has no practical consequence |
| H04 — Teaching can preserve or lose knowledge | Teacher/student access, time, proficiency, teaching evidence | Demonstrate, practice, mentor, reject teaching | Student capability and later innovations; possible loss | Instant universal teaching or guaranteed inheritance |
| H05 — Trust and governance modify commons outcomes | Rules-in-use, rights, knowledge, monitoring, local interests | Agree, enforce, sanction, revise, negotiate | Resource stocks, compliance, disputed use and legitimacy | One universal "trust score" replaces specific relations |

| H07 — Conflict may escalate or resolve | Claims, credible threats, asymmetric information, obligations, force | Bargain, mobilize, intimidate, yield, fight | Causal warnings, failed offers, actual recruits and damage | Automatic war once "tension" reaches a threshold |

For every accepted hypothesis, eventually record: world/individual authority, indexes, read/write pattern, cadence, performance cost, save/restore requirements, causal validation, seeded counterfactual and player-facing discoverability. **None are implementation instructions yet.**

## 5. Five controlled kingdom experiments to build after foundations permit

Experiments compare the **same world seed, relevant history seed(s), and deterministic rule version**; interventions must be explicit inputs with provenance, not hand-authored story events. Do not change many parameters simultaneously and pretend to know the cause.

### X01 — The prepared farmer versus a vulnerable harvest

**Baseline:** Two households in the same climate region, differing in real irrigation, crop diversity, tools, seed stores, labor and relevant knowledge.  
**Intervention:** A reproducible reduction in rainfall; separate tests with storage, drainage or transport improvements.  
**Observe:** Harvest mass by resource, food consumption, reserves, debt, purchase/sale flows, reliance on family/neighbors, health, survival, employment and migration.  
**Pass criterion:** Material and personal outcomes respond to interventions in a causally explainable way; no guaranteed survival or collapse.  
**Fail signal:** Only an abstract morale/stat changes while goods, named households and dependencies remain static.

### X02 — The death of the only trained specialist

**Baseline:** A town depends on a named miller, healer, boat builder, irrigation mechanic or other actually needed expert.  
**Intervention:** Remove the specialist through an explicit death event; compare to earlier apprenticeship or accessible replacement capacity.  
**Observe:** Services interrupted, unmet demand, successor training, knowledge loss, reassignments, wages, cost, travel and institutional responses.  
**Pass criterion:** Competence, training and substitutes determine downstream changes; "important NPC death" is not a scripted exception.  
**Fail signal:** The service works unchanged because a generic shop token still exists.

### X03 — Two communities sharing water

**Baseline:** Both use the same upstream/downstream water flow; rights, trust, local food stakes, response capacity and leadership differ by seeded circumstances.  
**Intervention:** Same drought in distinct runs with clear rights/mediation versus uncertain rights/weak enforcement; vary information accuracy separately.  
**Observe:** Withdrawals, crop effects, negotiations, promises, willingness to retaliate, patrols, actual logistics and resolution.  
**Pass criterion:** Both cooperation and conflict are possible through mechanisms, with traceable paths.  
**Fail signal:** Drought always triggers war or a hidden peace quota suppresses rational decisions.

### X04 — An adventuring party learns—or falls apart

**Baseline:** Several unrelated individuals with heterogeneous capabilities, priorities, trust and social knowledge.  
**Intervention:** Shared training/expeditions, then the death or departure of a central member in one branch; compare with no shared training.  
**Observe:** Who coordinates with whom, actual task outcomes, injuries, loyalty, disagreements, redistributed leadership and continuance.  
**Pass criterion:** Shared experience and ties affect outcomes, and no group is automatically loyal forever.  
**Fail signal:** Group label creates competence/obedience regardless of what members experienced.

### X05 — Merchant failure during seasonal shortage

**Baseline:** Named merchants, carts/boats, road conditions, goods, buyers, debts, insurance/guarantors where real, farmers and restaurants dependent on delivery.  
**Intervention:** Equipment failure or caravan delay; compare maintained wagons, trustworthy partner or alternate route.  
**Observe:** Actual delivery, prices, replacement procurement, changed menus, tool availability, wages, credit, bankruptcies and relationships.  
**Pass criterion:** Preparation and network redundancy matter, without inventing replacement goods or vendors.  
**Fail signal:** Merchant "fails" in a narrative log but everyone still receives all contracted supplies.

## 6. Instrumentation: judge causality and playability, not drama counts

Suggested **research-stage** observables, each subject to cost and validity checks:

- **Basic material closure:** Produced/imported/transferred/consumed/stored/destroyed stocks reconcile by source, destination and time. No unexplained creation or disappearance of vital goods.
- **Population process:** Age distribution, living births/deaths, kin-group continuity, household formation, migration and vulnerability to common shocks.
- **Labor and dependence:** Actual delivered services by specialty and the consequences of an unavailable skilled worker.
- **Institutional resilience:** Claims processed, resources governed, succession attempts, enforcement and disputes, including peaceful settlements.
- **Agency traceability:** Who attempted what for what actor-known reasons, what actual resources or relationships constrained them, and what changed.
- **Knowledge transmission:** Verified teaching encounters, learned capability, lost practices, institutional records and later rediscovery.
- **Conflict pathway:** Claims, mobilization, failed/successful negotiations, force size, casualties and material aftermath. Zero battles is not automatically a failure.
- **Player discoverability:** Whether consequential history can be encountered through valid local knowledge, conversation, trade, observation or records. Offscreen events may remain unknown.
- **Diversity across seeds:** Survival, development, failures, peaceful periods, creative activity and institutional variation; look for homogeneous/sterile outcomes without imposing cinematic quotas.
- **Engineering overhead:** CPU per simulated year, event growth, cached/resident state, cold-read budget, bounded decision candidates, snapshot/restore fidelity and determinism.

**Zero-story-quotas rule:** Never optimize the number of wars, romances, miracles or dramatic climaxes as a mandatory per-seed score. Optimize causal coherence, encounterable opportunities, meaningful action, stable continuity, and variation for defensible reasons.

## 7. Questions this first tranche cannot honestly answer

1. **How many farms and how much cultivated land feed 1,500?** Depends on crops, yields, season length, terrain, water, spoilage, working population, diet, livestock, transport, tools and magic; need a biome/production model before quantitative staffing.
2. **How many specialists can be supported?** Depends on labor productivity, surplus, demand, trade and access, not one historical occupation percentage.
3. **What makes this a kingdom instead of a federation, chiefdom, city-state or collection of villages?** Requires political/cultural history and a feasible number of rulers, retainers, clerks, guards and local institutions. No universal cutoff.
4. **What share can fight?** Requires actual age, biology, jobs, obligations, material supply, training, rank, coercion and willingness. A 100–200-person event is plausible only *conditionally*.
5. **Can the population sustain 1,000 years without unjustified outside supply?** Requires a full demographic/material ecology experiment, not confidence drawn from an archive benchmark alone.
6. **What counts as "love" or a credible relationship?** Need separate research on bonding, partnership, caregiving, attachment, culture, mutual consent and relational development; avoid simplistic attraction meters.
7. **Why do some people become unusually capable?** Must weigh individual traits, deliberate practice, cumulative experience, chance, social access, health, mentors, resources and domain difficulty; no single "greatness" statistic.
8. **How should magical ranks alter farming, medicine, labor, deterrence and institutional power?** Must consult rank ecology and domain rules instead of treating magic as flavor.
9. **Which theories transfer to our setting?** Evidence is mainly human and often culturally/time-bound; nonhuman physiology, magic and seeded historical alternatives need explicit design constraints.
10. **Which mechanisms can run cheaply?** Only measurements against actual ATE data structures can establish bounds; don't design a universal high-frequency cognition loop.
11. **What are appropriate external boundaries?** A 1,500-person kingdom cannot quietly rely on abstract infinite neighbors or free imported goods. Define any neighboring interactions precisely.
12. **What is a good public-facing activity loop?** Civilization plausibility does not itself prove gameplay quality. The vertical slice must make choices and consequences enjoyable, comprehensible and discoverable.

## 8. Next research work packages, prioritized

**R-A — Material household model and kingdom topology (next):** Compare historical/economic work on agrarian household production, seasonal labor, storage, water, trade, non-elite consumption, and political organization. Deliver a provisional dependency graph and **bounded input ranges only where sourced**. Trace what current ATE actually models versus what is missing.

**R-B — Individual motivation, development and relationships:** Distinguish motives, needs, aspiration, opportunity, trust, identity and competence; review multiple theories and cross-cultural limits. Design hypotheses for independent life goals and meaningful player release.

**R-C — Group competence, leadership and institutional continuity:** Examine collective action, reciprocity, governance, mentorship, succession, power, corruption and dissolution, including disagreements in the literature.

**R-D — Conflict, war prevention and small-population mobilization:** Research raiding, feuds, coercion, coalition formation, logistics, diplomacy, bargaining failure and peacekeeping at village/kingdom scale—not empire-sized wars.

**R-E — Social/ecological shocks and long-run civilization:** Study variation in hazards, vulnerability, demography, social memory, migration, trade dependency and economic/institutional recovery.

**R-F — Convert verified findings into tests:** Review the existing ATE domain schema and Stage 1 contracts; map the highest-value causes to minimal data, simulation cadence and counterfactual tests before any coding authorization.

**Research boundary:** These packages can be developed independently while Sol finishes Stage 0.5; implementation must respect the repository's actual architecture and the owner's gate decisions.
